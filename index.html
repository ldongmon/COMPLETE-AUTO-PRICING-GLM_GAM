1. Introduction

This project builds the tools a pricing actuary uses to answer a common question in personal motor insurance: a segment of the book has a Loss Ratio above the rest; should we reprice it, by how much, and what would that do to the business? It follows the full chain such a decision requires: data quality checks, exploratory analysis, frequency and severity modeling (GLM and GAM, compared out of sample), a technical premium, portfolio diagnostics, retention and new-business conversion scenarios, a commercial premium, a repricing scenario engine with a multi-year outlook, and an interactive dashboard.

Every segment conclusion is checked on data the models were not fitted on. Two first-pass signals did not survive that check: a large region whose high Loss Ratio came from a few very large claims, and a fuel-type gap that came from a defect in the data file. The segment finally retained shows the same gap in the training, validation and test sets.

2. Business Context

A personal motor insurer prices policies with rating factors (driver age, vehicle, location, bonus-malus) to estimate each policy's expected cost, the technical premium. Some segments can drift away from their actual risk: a segment that looked adequately priced when the tariff was built can become a source of loss. Finding such a segment, and deciding what to do about it, is routine work for a pricing team. Getting it wrong means either losing money on an under-priced segment or losing customers on an over-priced one.

The project reproduces that workflow on a public, single-year cross-section of a French motor third-party-liability book. The dataset has real limits: no charged premium, no expense data, no renewal or quote history. The project states how each gap is handled rather than working around it silently.

3. Problem Statement

The business question:
Segment X has a Loss Ratio above the rest of the book. Should we reprice it, by how much, and what would that do to retention, new-business conversion and portfolio profitability?

Answering it requires: (1) a reliable estimate of each policy's expected cost (frequency × severity), (2) a way to tell a segment that is truly under-priced from one that only has bad luck in its claims, (3) a view of how customers react if the price changes, and (4) a single recommendation that combines the three.

Why 9,387 policies are excluded. Every policy at the start of the file, up to IDpol 24,951, carries a reported claim: there is no claim-free policy in that range, the implied frequency is above 200%, and only 3.1% of those claims have a payment in the severity file. Elsewhere, 96% of policies are claim-free and 99.9% of claims are paid. This block is a claims extract, not part of the insured population. Kept, it inflated the modeled frequency without the matching cost, and distorted relativities, fuel type in particular.

6. Phase-by-Phase Walkthrough

Every notebook below is executed with its outputs; the "Notebook" links open the code and results on GitHub.

Phase 1-2 · Data Quality & Exploratory Analysis Notebook 01 · Notebook 02

What data do we have, can we trust it, and which factors move frequency and severity?

The reported claims without payment are not spread across the file: almost all of them sit in a leading claims-only block, which is excluded. Exposure-weighted claim frequency is 7.40%; the plain average of ClaimNb (3.9%) is a number of claims per policy, not a frequency, because most policies are observed for part of the year. BonusMalus is the strongest frequency factor (5.2% at the bonus floor to 61% above 160). Area duplicates Density. Frequency and severity are driven by different factors, which supports modeling them separately.

Phase 3 · Data Preparation & Split Notebook 03

How do we build a leak-free train/validation/test split?

A 60/20/20 split at policy level, stratified on claim count. Frequency is 7.40-7.41% on the three sets and the median paid claim is EUR 1,172 on all three; mean severity differs only because a few very large claims fell in the validation set.

Phase 4 · Frequency Model: Poisson GLM Notebook 04

What is the expected claim count per policy?

Exposure used as a log offset, never as a feature. Predicted frequency ranks the validation set correctly, from 3.8% in the first decile to 17.9% in the last. DrivAge's multivariate coefficient is positive while its univariate trend is negative: confounding with BonusMalus (r = -0.48), reported as such.

Phase 5 · Frequency Model: GAM Benchmark Notebook 05

Do non-linear effects improve on the linear GLM?

Yes, out of sample: pseudo-R² 5.49% vs. 4.53%, Gini 0.311 vs. 0.285. The in-sample comparison would have suggested a 2.5x gap and is not used. The partial dependence curves show a DrivAge trough near 30 and a rise after 75, a VehAge plateau up to 10 years then a decline, and a concave BonusMalus effect.

Phase 6 · Severity Model: Gamma GLM & GAM Notebook 06

How large is a claim, given it happened?

Neither model improves meaningfully on a naive average out of sample (pseudo-R²: naive -0.11%, GLM -0.09%, GAM +0.04%). The rating factors differentiate claim occurrence, not claim size. Claims above EUR 15,000 are charged as a flat loading of EUR 33.54 per policy-year.

Phase 7 · Technical Premium (GAM-informed Refined GLM) Notebook 07

What is the expected claims cost per policy?

DrivAge (6 bins) and VehAge (4 bins) are re-binned from the GAM curves: validation pseudo-R² 4.72%, Gini 0.295, while staying a plain GLM rate table. The technical premium reconciles with paid claims on the training set (99.7%) and the test set (98.3%).

Phase 8 · Portfolio KPIs: Finding the Segment Notebook 08

Which segment is under-priced, and is the gap real?

A raw Loss Ratio scan flags Region R24 (146.7%, 28.5% of exposure); on the attritional layer it is in line with the book (100.8% vs. 100.5%), its raw figure coming from large losses. Fuel type shows no stable gap: Diesel and Regular have the same rate of large claims. The one gap that holds on the training, validation and test sets is BonusMalus 51-80 (28.3% of exposure), which costs 15% to 25% more than its technical premium predicts. Cause: the GLM's straight-line BonusMalus term against a concave effect; with BonusMalus in bands, validation pseudo-R² rises from 4.72% to 5.28%. Out-of-sample cost relativity: 1.17.

Phase 9 · Retention (Renewal) Simulator Notebook 09

If we reprice the segment, how many existing customers do we lose?

A documented simulator, not a fitted model: the dataset has no renewal history. Its tenure input comes from the French bonus-malus formula. At +17%: about 4.8% of the segment's policies (about 10,100) are projected to lapse.

Phase 10 · Conversion (New Business) Simulator Notebook 10

If we quote above the market, how many prospects still convert?

Lower baseline (15% vs. 88% for renewal) and larger price elasticity. At +17%: conversion falls from 15.0% to 6.0%, assuming competitors price the segment like today's tariff.

Phase 11 · Commercial Premium & Scenario Engine Notebook 11

What correction, and with what effect on profitability?

The commercial loading (37%) covers the 27% expense assumption on a technical premium equal to expected cost; the loading and the scenario engine use the same cost basis, so the whole book at a 0% change has a contribution of zero. At current prices the segment's expected contribution is about EUR -5.7M a year. Over -10% to +30%, contribution improves steadily; the engine's own optimum (+95%) is outside the range its assumptions cover and is not a recommendation. Indicated correction: +17%, equal to the measured cost relativity.

7. Interactive Dashboard

The models are wired into a three-page Streamlit app. Each numeric input pairs a slider with an exact-value box, so a figure such as BonusMalus = 137 can be typed directly.

Pricing Simulator

Enter a client's profile (driver age, vehicle, Bonus-Malus, region) and get the technical and commercial premium, broken down into expected frequency, severity and large-loss loading.

Retention & Conversion

See how a price change moves an existing customer's renewal probability or a prospect's probability of accepting a quote, with the curve over the whole price range.

Portfolio Scenario

Pick a segment (whole book, Bonus-Malus band, fuel type, region or brand) and a price change, and project Loss Ratio, COR, ATP and contribution 1, 2 or 3 years out.

9. Conclusion

BonusMalus 51-80 should be repriced by about +17%. In tariff terms, this is a correction of the BonusMalus relativity for that band, and the next tariff revision should model BonusMalus in bands rather than as a straight line. The conclusion rests on a gap measured on data the models were not fitted on, after ruling out a region whose Loss Ratio came from large losses and a fuel-type gap that came from a defect in the data file.

The retention and conversion effects come from simulators calibrated on literature values, not on observed price response, which this dataset cannot support. The correction would be applied with a test-and-learn cell, and the simulators recalibrated on the observed experience. The Streamlit app lets a pricing team run the same analysis on another segment or another price change without touching the code.
